\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Language models are increasingly asked not only to answer questions but to recommend actions, and to justify each recommendation with citations to evidence supplied in the prompt. A rationale of this kind makes three distinct promises: that the recommended action is good, that what the rationale says about the evidence is true, and that the cited evidence is what the recommendation rests on. Evaluations usually check at most one of them. Work on attribution asks whether generated statements are supported by their sources~\cite{Rashkin2023AIS,Gao2023ALCE,Min2023FActScore,Liu2023Verifiability}, typically for question answering or summarization, where no downstream action is scored. Work on LLM decision-making scores the action~\cite{Yang2024OPRO,Lao2024GPTuner,Giannakouris2025LambdaTune} but treats the rationale as commentary. Neither asks what the evidence alone would have recommended.

We study these promises in configuration advice, an application where LLMs read manuals and measurements and propose database or kernel settings with natural-language justifications~\cite{Trummer2022DBBERT,Lao2024GPTuner,Giannakouris2025LambdaTune,Chen2024AutoOS,Lin2025TuneAgent}. The setting is unusually well suited to auditing grounded rationales. The evidence is small, structured and numeric, so most claims about it can be checked exactly rather than by judging entailment. The quality of a decision can be measured on held-out runs, independently of the model and of any mechanism that enforces its advice. And the evidence can be handed unchanged to a conventional selector, which says what the evidence determines without a language model.

We ask three questions. \emph{Added value}: does the model choose better than a conventional selector that receives the same evidence, or does it only repackage that evidence? \emph{Explanation correctness}: when an explanation cites valid evidence, does it state that evidence correctly? \emph{Reliance}: does the cited evidence actually influence the decision? To answer them we build \sysname, a testbed that separates evidence (a typed interaction graph and retrieved measurement traces), proposals (an LLM that emits a configuration with citations and an explanation) and enforcement (a runtime that validates, vetoes or stages the proposal). Because the evidence is an explicit, frozen object, it can be given to a non-LLM selector, ablated, or partially deleted; because enforcement is separate, its errors can be counted against labels computed from raw measurements.

Auditing ten LLMs, from 7B open-weight to hosted frontier models, on real Linux timer-slack and CPU-affinity interventions, we find that the three promises come apart. Evidence is necessary: without it, nine models mostly choose a worse configuration, and the local models we audited justify that choice by inventing meanings for opaque configuration names. It is also sufficient: with it, nine models reproduce the choice of a selector that simply reads the training table, and none improves on it. Citations are valid but one-sided and front-loaded: 252 of 260 cited retrieval runs belong to the chosen configuration, and 209 come from the first half of the evidence list. Explanations are cited but not correct (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}): 16 of 28 audited claims from local models and 25 of 69 from hosted models misreport values, ranges or units, attribute a value to the wrong source, or overstate a regularity, and most of these accompany a correct decision. Deleting cited evidence reveals citation-specific reliance for only one model.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i)~An audit protocol for evidence-grounded LLM advice that combines a matched-information non-LLM baseline, evidence-source ablations, cited versus matched uncited deletion, claim-level verification of explanations against the supplied evidence, and outcome labels independent of enforcement, with all data roles frozen before test data are opened (\S\ref{sec:protocol}).
(ii)~An audit of ten LLMs reported with every denominator, including an analysis of what models cite and a claim-level taxonomy of explanation errors (\S\ref{sec:results}).
(iii)~The \sysname testbed, whose calibrated risk gate provides a cautionary result on selective prediction under shift, with prompts, raw responses, per-claim annotations, code and measurement logs provided as supplementary material (\S\ref{sec:testbed}).

The task is deliberately small, one workload family on one host, and its default configuration is best, so the audit can reveal failures of added value but not successes; we make no LLM-superiority, deployment-efficacy or production-safety claim (see Limitations).
