\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{Decisions and rationales come apart.}
Most misstatements accompany a correct choice. This pattern is consistent with a decision driven by a simple reading of the evidence, here the minimum of the training table, and a rationale that is generated as a separate summary of the retrieval runs. Choosing the smallest of four means is easy; summarizing forty noisy measurements with ranges, extremes and quantifiers is where the errors concentrate. Because the decision is right, an end-to-end evaluation would never surface these errors, and a reader who trusts the rationale could come away with a false picture of the evidence, for instance that the chosen configuration is never beaten. This mirrors findings that explanations can be plausible yet unfaithful to what drives a prediction~\cite{Jacovi2020Faithfulness,Turpin2023Unfaithful}, with the difference that here the error is checkable against the prompt itself.

\paragraph{Citations as selective presentation.}
A citation list is also an editorial choice. Nine of ten models cited only runs of the configuration they chose, and most cited the first runs in the list, so a reader who follows the citations sees supporting evidence only and never the blocks in which a competitor wins. It also licenses overstatement (\S\ref{subsec:claims}): ``consistently the lowest'' cannot be contradicted by the cited runs, only by those left uncited. The six-citation cap explains the preference for early items, which follows list position, but not the preference for confirming ones, which persists without it (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

\paragraph{Scale reduces but does not remove misstatement.}
Every audited model misstated at least two claims, and ``consistently the lowest'' recurs across families. On full-context prompts, misstatement falls with size among open-weight models (7/8 claims at 7--15B, 21/52 at 70--671B; Spearman $\rho=-0.78$ over eight models, a trend on few claims; Appendix~\ref{app:scale}) yet stays at 17--50\% per hosted model; unit and source errors occur only at small scale, overstatements and wrong values at every scale. Only Gemini-Pro cites other configurations. The superlative, range and quantifier errors long documented in data-to-text generation~\cite{Thomson2020GoldStandard,Chen2020LogicNLG} are therefore not removed by instruction tuning or scale in the models we tested.

\paragraph{Implications for attribution metrics.}
Attribution benchmarks ask whether each cited passage supports its statement, often with an entailment judge~\cite{Rashkin2023AIS,Gao2023ALCE}. One-sided citation lists are invisible to such per-citation scores, and many misstated claims quote correct numbers and err only in a comparison or quantifier, near-misses that eight judges, including the fact-checkers AlignScore and MiniCheck, fail to separate from correct claims (balanced accuracy 0.47--0.68; Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}). Evaluations should therefore also measure what is left out, such as the share of contrary evidence cited, and check claims exactly where possible.

\paragraph{What deletion can and cannot show.}
Matched deletion measures whether a decision is sensitive to what is cited, which is a form of self-consistency rather than faithfulness to internal computation~\cite{Parcalabescu2024SelfConsistency}. Even that is estimable only when the evidence contains redundant items of the same kind: half of our models cited the single training table, for which no control exists. Evidence sets for such audits should be designed with matched alternatives for every item a model might cite.

\paragraph{When could an LLM add value?}
Our evidence determines the best choice, so a selector that reads it is hard to beat. A language model could add value when the evidence is incomplete and prior knowledge, for example from documentation, carries information the measurements lack. Testing this requires tasks in which the default is not optimal and in which the baseline receives the same documents; the invented label meanings in our no-evidence runs show why such priors must be checked, not assumed.
