\begin{abstract}
Language models increasingly recommend actions and justify them with citations to evidence in the prompt. Such a rationale makes three promises: the action is good, its claims about the evidence are true, and the decision rests on what is cited. Evaluations rarely test all three, and almost never against what the evidence alone would recommend. We propose an audit protocol that gives an equally informed non-LLM selector the same frozen evidence, ablates and deletes evidence to probe necessity and reliance, verifies every quantitative claim in an explanation against that evidence, and scores decisions on independently labeled held-out outcomes. We apply it to configuration advice on real Linux kernel interventions, using SemantOS, a testbed that separates evidence, proposals and enforcement, and ten LLMs from 7B open-weight to hosted frontier models. The promises come apart. Evidence is necessary: without it, nine models mostly choose a worse configuration, and local models invent meanings for opaque configuration names. It is also sufficient: with it, nine models reproduce a table-lookup selector's choice and none improves on it. Explanations are cited but not correct: 41 of 97 audited claims misstate the evidence they validly cite, most alongside a correct decision, and deletion reveals citation-specific reliance for only one model. We release prompts, raw responses, per-claim annotations and logs.
\end{abstract}
