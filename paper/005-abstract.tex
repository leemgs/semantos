\begin{abstract}
LLMs increasingly recommend actions and justify them by citing evidence in the prompt. Such rationales make three promises: the action is good, their claims about the evidence are true, and the decision rests on what is cited. Evaluations rarely test all three or use an evidence-only baseline. Our audit protocol gives an equally informed non-LLM selector the same frozen evidence, ablates and deletes evidence, verifies each explanation's quantitative claims against it, and scores decisions on independently labeled held-out outcomes. We apply it to configuration advice on Linux kernel interventions with \sysname, a testbed separating evidence from enforcement, and ten LLMs from 7B to frontier scale, with database settings as a second domain. The promises come apart. In the kernel task, evidence is necessary: without it, nine models mostly choose worse; with it, nine reproduce the optimal choice of a table-lookup selector. Citations are valid but one-sided: 252 of 260 cited runs belong to the chosen configuration. Explanations are cited but not correct: 41 of 97 audited claims misstate the evidence, mostly alongside a correct decision. In a database task with conflicting measurements, most models leave the selector's choice and misreport contrary evidence. Citation-specific reliance under deletion is rare and unstable.
\end{abstract}
